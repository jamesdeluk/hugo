## Purpose

This is the working model for the Relationship vault: a successful long-term romantic relationship is a secure, living partnership between two adults. It needs shared direction, responsive connection, deliberate maintenance, constructive repair, trustworthy agreements and an ability to learn from its actual experience.

The model is organised as connected parts rather than a single doctrine. Together, they form a usable structure for building, maintaining and assessing a relationship.

## Shared life and partnership

### Purpose

A relationship is a chosen shared life between two adults. It needs sufficient alignment on the questions that shape that life, and a way of making decisions that protects both people and the partnership.

### Shared direction

Shared direction does not mean identical preferences, personalities or interests. It means that the partners can build a life both genuinely want. Questions about commitment, children, money, location, work, family, sex, privacy and time have practical consequences; leaving them implicit turns them into future sources of conflict.

Some differences can be accommodated through flexibility or a good division of responsibility. Others would require one person to surrender something fundamental. The purpose of early and ongoing discussion is to know which is which.

### Good-enough partnership

No partner will be perfectly easy, fully understood or permanently aligned in desire, mood and preference. Compatibility is not a frictionless precondition; it is partly built through humility, tolerance for difference, practical cooperation and a willingness to keep learning each other.

Domestic work, care, money, logistics and repair are therefore not distractions from love. They are among the ordinary ways two people make a shared life reliable.

### Equal partnership

The relationship is neither an escape from individual responsibility nor a competition between individual interests. Each person has legitimate boundaries, ambitions, friendships, needs and an inner life. At the same time, both people have responsibility for the conditions in which the relationship can thrive.

Equal partnership is experienced in the ordinary distribution of power: who decides, whose time matters, how money is handled, whose preferences set the default and whether either person repeatedly benefits at the other’s expense.

### Agreements rather than assumptions

An agreement makes an expectation visible, mutual and revisable. It does not need to make the relationship bureaucratic. It prevents partners from discovering only during a rupture that they were relying on different private rules.

Useful agreement areas include:

- commitment and exclusivity;
- privacy, disclosure and digital life;
- time together, time apart and friendships;
- money, work, home and practical responsibility;
- sex, affection and how either person raises unmet needs;
- family, children, location and longer-term direction.

### Review the relationship’s design

An arrangement that suited one stage of a relationship may stop working as work, health, family, finances, desire or life ambitions change. Partners need to revisit their agreements before the relationship becomes organised around an outdated default.

The review includes power as well as preference: whether either person’s dignity, agency, privacy or voice has been steadily reduced in order to preserve the arrangement. A durable design allows flexibility without making one person bear the cost of every change.

### Practices

- Discuss major life questions before a decision is urgent.
- Name whether something is a preference, a strong need or a non-negotiable condition.
- Make decisions in terms both people experience as fair, then revisit them when circumstances change.
- Notice where one person is silently carrying disproportionate emotional, practical or financial responsibility.
- Use regular conversations to update the shared picture of the future.
- Review the relationship’s arrangements after a meaningful life change, not only after a major rupture.

### Questions

- What are we building together in practical rather than aspirational terms?
  For example, whether both people want a home, children, ambitious work or a particular place to live.
- Which differences can we accommodate well, and which would create a structural conflict?
  For example, one person wanting children and the other not wanting them.
- Where are we relying on an assumption instead of an agreement?
  For example, assuming how weekends, spending or contact with former partners will work without discussing it.
- How does each person experience fairness, agency and influence in the relationship?
  For example, whether one person’s work schedule or family preferences routinely set the default.
- What would need updating if work, health, family or financial circumstances changed?
  For example, revisiting domestic responsibilities after a promotion, illness or move.

## Security and attachment

### Purpose

A secure relationship gives both people a dependable bond without requiring either to abandon selfhood. It makes closeness and independence mutually reinforcing: the relationship becomes a secure base from which each person can live more freely.

### Attachment under stress

Stress can make an ordinary cue feel like criticism, rejection, distance or loss of control. Each person then uses familiar protective moves: pursuing, withdrawing, criticising, pleasing, defending or shutting down. The partner experiences that protection as a new threat and responds in kind. Soon both people are fighting the loop rather than addressing the original event.

Attachment patterns describe learned expectations about closeness and safety. They are useful when they help someone recognise a pattern and choose a different response; the important work is what happens next.

### The pursue-withdraw cycle

One common version of this loop is pursuit and withdrawal. One partner experiences distance and protests it through criticism, urgency or repeated demands for contact. The other experiences that protest as pressure or failure and withdraws further. Both are trying to protect the bond, but their protective moves increase the very isolation each fears.

The way out is vulnerable reaching: replace the protest or shutdown with the more exposed truth underneath it. “I miss you”, “I am scared we are losing closeness” or “I need to know I matter to you” gives a partner something they can respond to.

### Responsiveness and bids

Security is built through responsiveness in small moments. A bid for connection might be a question, a change in tone, an invitation, a request for reassurance or a wish to share something. Responding does not mean meeting every request immediately. It means treating the request and the person making it as significant.

Space also belongs in a secure relationship. The relevant question is whether space is communicated and reliable, rather than used to make the other person uncertain or to avoid the relationship.

### Comfort preferences

Comfort is not a single response. At different times a person may want to be heard, reassured, helped to think through options, given practical help, touched or simply left with some quiet company. Attempts to solve or reframe a problem when someone needs empathy can increase disconnection.

Partners can make their comfort preferences explicit while calm: what helps when one is anxious, ashamed, exhausted, grieving or overwhelmed, and what tends to make things worse.

### Returning to safety

When stress takes over, problem-solving tends to become worse. The immediate task is to restore enough safety that both people can think, listen and choose their next move.

1. Notice the early sign of escalation or withdrawal.
2. Name the state without assigning blame.
3. State the underlying need directly: reassurance, clarity, respect, space, affection, support or time to think.
4. If a pause is needed, agree when and how the conversation will resume.
5. Return to the issue as a shared problem.

### Practices

- Learn one another’s early signs of feeling unsafe, overwhelmed or disconnected.
- Replace mind-reading with direct requests.
- Respond to bids for connection often enough that the relationship feels dependable.
- Make space for separate interests, friends and reflection without creating ambiguity about commitment.
- After a trigger, discuss the interpretation that arose as well as the event itself.
- When pursuit or withdrawal appears, name the cycle as the problem and communicate the fear or need beneath the protective move.
- Respond to vulnerable reaching with reassurance and curiosity before returning to practical solutions.

### Questions

- What does each of us tend to fear or assume under stress?
  For example, “you are pulling away because I do not matter” or “I am about to be criticised again”.
- Which protective move is each of us most likely to make?
  For example, pursuing with repeated messages, becoming critical, going quiet or becoming overly self-reliant.
- What helps each of us regain enough safety to think clearly?
  For example, a hug, ten quiet minutes, reassurance that the conversation will resume or a walk together.
- Which bids for connection do we regularly miss or misread?
  For example, hearing “do you want to watch something?” as a logistical question rather than a wish for closeness.
- How can we ask for space in a way that preserves connection?
  For example, “I need half an hour to settle, then I will come back and we can talk at eight.”
- What would vulnerable reaching sound like for each of us?
  For example, “I am angry, but beneath that I am scared that we are losing closeness.”
- How does each of us most want to be comforted when distressed?
  For example, listening and holding hands before offering any practical advice.

## Communication and empathy

### Purpose

Communication is how partners make their inner worlds, needs, appreciation and requests available to one another. The aim is not perfect wording. It is enough honesty and empathy that both people can understand what is happening and work with it together.

### From judgement to connection

When a partner is upset, language often jumps straight to a verdict: “you do not care”, “you are selfish”, “you never listen”. The verdict may point to a real pain, but it gives the other person a character to defend rather than an experience to understand.

A more useful sequence is:

1. **Observation:** what happened, stated specifically and without an assessment of character or motive.
2. **Feeling:** the emotion the event brought up.
3. **Need:** the value, wish or condition that matters beneath the feeling.
4. **Request:** a concrete action the other person can accept, decline or adapt.

For example, replace “you never make time for me” with: “When we have not had time together this week, I feel disconnected because I value closeness and shared time. Would you be willing to choose one evening for us this week?”

### Facts, stories and candour

Two people can encounter the same event and construct different stories about its meaning. Treating the story as a fact makes candour sound like a verdict; treating it as an interpretation makes it possible to reveal oneself without requiring the other person to agree.

Candour is sharing what is happening on one’s own “planet” — the thought, interpretation, want or fear — in order to be known. It needs conscious listening alongside it: each person makes room for the other’s information before deciding what it means.

Useful language includes: “The fact I noticed was …; the story I started telling myself was …; I want to check it with you.”

### Emotional translation

People sometimes communicate a vulnerable need through a less vulnerable form: criticism, control, passive aggression, indifference or a demand. Emotional translation listens for the fear, longing or wish underneath the surface message and then brings it into direct language.

Translation is an invitation, not an assertion about what a partner “really” means. Offer it tentatively — “Are you worried that …?” — and let the partner correct it. The aim is to turn an indirect bid into an explicit request for reassurance, closeness, support or space.

### Hear before solving

Empathy means staying with the other person’s experience long enough for them to feel understood. Listen for the event they are responding to, the feeling it created, the need beneath it and the request they may be struggling to express.

This is different from agreeing with every interpretation. It is also different from correcting, explaining, reassuring, advising or moving quickly to a solution. Those responses can follow after understanding has been established.

Useful responses include:

- “It sounds as though that left you feeling dismissed, because being considered matters to you.”
- “Have I understood that you want more clarity about what we agreed?”
- “Is there more you want me to understand before we work out what to do?”

### Requests that preserve autonomy

A useful request is specific, positive and possible to answer. “Be more loving” or “respect me” may name an important need, but they do not tell a partner what action would meet it. A request becomes a demand when refusal is met with blame, guilt, punishment or withdrawal.

The relationship gains trust when both people can make requests directly and can say no honestly. A no is not the end of the conversation; it is information about another need, limit or concern that needs understanding before a different strategy can be found.

### Communication under stress

When a person is overwhelmed, even good language can become mechanical or unavailable. In those moments:

1. pause and recognise that clear listening is no longer available;
2. state the immediate need plainly without blame;
3. agree how and when to return;
4. give self-empathy or seek enough space to return constructively;
5. resume with observation, feeling, need and request.

This gives the relationship a way to handle intensity without converting it into attack, withdrawal or a false resolution.

### Appreciation

Specific appreciation sustains goodwill. Rather than a general compliment, name the action, the need it met and the positive effect it had. This lets a partner understand what they contributed and allows both people to celebrate it without turning appreciation into a tool for control.

### Practices

- Turn complaints into an observation, feeling, need and request before raising them.
- Ask a partner to reflect back what they heard when the subject is important or emotionally loaded.
- Lead with empathy before advice or explanation.
- Respond to a no by understanding the need behind it, then look for another strategy.
- Make appreciation specific and describe its impact.
- Notice when a judgment is carrying an unspoken need, then express the need directly.
- Separate the observable fact from the story being made about it, then offer the story as an interpretation rather than a conclusion.

### Questions

- What is the observable event here, separate from my interpretation of it?
  For example, “we did not speak after dinner yesterday”, rather than “you were ignoring me”.
- What do I feel, and what need does that reveal?
  For example, “I feel lonely because I need connection and attention.”
- What exact action am I asking for?
  For example, “Would you be willing to put phones away for twenty minutes after dinner?”
- Has my partner had enough room to say no honestly?
  For example, whether they could suggest a different evening without being treated as uncaring.
- Before solving this, do both of us feel understood?
  For example, each person can state the other’s concern in a way the other recognises.

## Maintenance, attraction and aliveness

### Purpose

Relationships need deliberate maintenance. Goodwill, attraction and a sense of aliveness are sustained through attention, curiosity, appreciation, affection, physical intimacy and shared experience — especially after novelty fades and ordinary life takes more space.

### The drift to prevent

Relationships often weaken through small, unaddressed disconnections rather than one decisive event. Partners become efficient at logistics but less attentive to one another’s experience. Appreciation becomes assumed, curiosity declines, irritations stay underground and emotional or sexual distance starts to feel normal.

This is why maintenance matters before there is a visible problem. It keeps the relationship from becoming administratively functional but emotionally undernourished.

### Attraction and compatibility

Chemistry establishes attraction; it does not establish whether a relationship can work. Compatibility becomes clearer through repeated interaction: how the two people enjoy ordinary life, handle difference, make repairs and develop a bond that is distinctive to them.

Attraction also changes with attention. Feeling known, valued and chosen; sharing meaningful activity; maintaining affection and sexual connection; and continuing to discover one another all give the relationship conditions in which desire can remain alive.

### Desire, goodwill and capacity

Emotional safety and sexual connection are linked: it is harder to remain open to affection or desire when the relationship feels unsafe, neglected or adversarial. At the same time, desire needs more than safety. It is fed by vitality, play, imagination, attention and the sense that a partner remains a separate person to discover rather than a completed project to manage.

Goodwill is the relationship’s working reserve. Specific appreciation, care and positive shared experiences build it; they make ordinary frustrations easier to absorb without turning every disappointment into an account of what is wrong with the relationship.

### Practices

- Protect time for connection that is not dominated by logistics, screens or other demands.
- Keep learning the other person: what has changed, what matters now, what they are enjoying and what feels difficult.
- Express appreciation specifically and often enough that it becomes ordinary language.
- Create shared experiences and rituals that belong to the relationship: play, exploration, affection, physical intimacy and recurring time together.
- Raise a small disappointment early, before it hardens into a story about the other person or the relationship.
- Notice when practical care, humour, touch or shared action would communicate more than another abstract discussion.
- Make a deliberate positive deposit after periods of stress: appreciation, affection, a shared experience or practical care that gives the other person more room to breathe.

### Questions

- When did we last give each other undivided attention?
  For example, an hour together without phones, errands or another task competing for attention.
- What makes this relationship feel distinctive and alive to each of us?
  For example, a shared interest, a private humour or an adventure neither person would choose alone.
- Where has familiarity become assumption?
  For example, assuming a partner already knows they are appreciated without saying so.
- What helps each of us feel appreciated, desired and chosen?
  For example, specific thanks, affectionate touch, initiating sex or protecting time together.
- Which shared rituals would add genuine connection rather than another obligation?
  For example, a weekly walk, a Sunday breakfast or a short end-of-day check-in.

## Conflict and repair

### Purpose

Conflict is inevitable; chronic disconnection is not. The aim is to turn friction into greater understanding and a more reliable relationship, rather than a repeated contest over who is right.

[Communication and empathy](<Communication and empathy.md>) provides the everyday language for making a conflict discussable: observation, feeling, need and request; full listening before problem-solving; and requests that preserve each person’s autonomy.

### The issue beneath the issue

The visible subject of an argument is often only part of what is happening. A disagreement about time, a message, money or household work can carry a deeper concern about respect, safety, fairness, closeness, autonomy or being understood.

The relationship moves forward when both people can say what the event meant to them, rather than continuing to fight only about the surface facts. A complaint is often a clumsy expression of a wish: more affection, more consideration, more reliability, more partnership or more room to breathe.

### The destructive loop

Small events become corrosive when they are converted into global claims, defensiveness, contempt, withdrawal or a catalogue of historic grievances. The original issue is then lost inside an escalating interaction.

When either person is too activated to listen or speak constructively, resolving the content immediately is rarely productive. The first task is to restore enough calm and connection to make a useful conversation possible.

### Capacity and the normal rhythm of repair

Good relationships do not sustain uninterrupted harmony. Their normal rhythm is closeness, disruption and a return to closeness. The relevant question after friction is not whether disruption happened, but whether both people know how to repair it.

Capacity matters. When one person is depleted, the other may be able to carry more for a while. When both are depleted, the relationship needs a joint plan to lower demands, add practical support and prevent exhaustion from becoming unkindness.

Before entering a difficult conversation, each person can state their current capacity. If the combined capacity is low, agree what can be postponed, simplified or supported, and when the conversation will be resumed.

### Repair sequence

1. Pause if either person cannot listen or speak constructively; agree when the conversation will resume.
2. Return to the specific event rather than assembling a case from the past.
3. Explain the impact and the meaning each person made of it.
4. Listen with curiosity; ask what the other person needed or wished had happened.
5. Take responsibility for the part you can own without first arguing about intent.
6. Make a concrete request or agreement for the future.
7. Reconnect deliberately, then check whether the agreement changed the recurring pattern.

### Four capacities of repair

- **Apologise:** own the harm without making self-protection the centre of the conversation.
- **Forgive:** recognise that a disappointing action does not make a person wholly bad, while still taking the breach seriously.
- **Teach:** explain what was missed and what would help next time with patience rather than punishment.
- **Learn:** receive feedback as information that can improve the relationship rather than a verdict on worth.

Recurring conflict often reflects an abandonment–control cycle. One partner experiences distance and attacks or pursues; the other feels pressured and withdraws. The conversation changes when each person explains the hurt or fear beneath their protective move rather than arguing from it.

### Practices

- Raise small issues early and specifically.
- Replace “always” and “never” with what happened, how it landed and what is needed now.
- Begin difficult conversations with appreciation or shared intent where it is genuine.
- Ask “tell me more” before explaining, correcting or defending.
- Apologise for impact cleanly; then change the condition that made repetition likely.
- If one approach is repeatedly failing, agree a different observable response and review whether it works.
- State current capacity before stress turns into criticism, withdrawal or contempt; make a joint plan when neither person has much to give.

### Questions

- What is this argument actually about for each of us?
  For example, an argument about late messages may be about reliability for one person and autonomy for the other.
- What is the wish beneath this complaint?
  For example, “you never help” may mean “I want us to share the household work more visibly.”
- What did I do that made it harder for my partner to feel understood or safe?
  For example, interrupting, rolling my eyes, becoming defensive or leaving without saying when I would return.
- What would a satisfactory repair look like to each person?
  For example, an apology, a calm conversation, physical reconnection and agreement about the next similar situation.
- What needs to change in practice, not merely be apologised for?
  For example, setting a reminder, changing a routine or agreeing how a difficult subject will be raised.

## Trust and boundaries

### Purpose

Trust is built through truthful, reliable behaviour and agreements that make the relationship’s boundaries clear. Good boundaries protect the partnership while leaving both people with dignity, autonomy and a legitimate life beyond it.

### Where ambiguity matters

Boundary questions tend to matter most where secrecy, divided intimacy or recurring insecurity can take hold. This can include digital communication, friendships, former partners, social media, privacy, money, travel, work relationships, time away and the way a partner handles external attention.

The right agreement is specific to the people and relationship involved. The important thing is that it is explicit, mutual and visible in behaviour.

### Jealousy and evidence

Jealousy is information that attention is needed; it is not a verdict about what is true or an instruction to control another person. A useful response distinguishes observable behaviour in the present from a recurring personal expectation of betrayal or abandonment.

Trust is strengthened by direct questions, clear commitment, respect and truthful conversation. Testing, surveillance, manufactured competition and repeated accusation create a relationship organised around insecurity.

### Practices

- Identify the areas where unspoken rules are likely to create ambiguity.
- Agree boundaries in positive, observable terms: what each person will do, disclose or protect.
- Raise a concern as a request for understanding and clarity, not an accusation that presumes a verdict.
- Discuss private contact or emotional intimacy outside the relationship before it becomes a secret.
- Review agreements after a breach or a major change in life circumstances.
- Make reliability visible through ordinary follow-through, not only through declarations.

### Questions

- Which boundaries protect the relationship without shrinking either person’s legitimate life?
  For example, being open about a close friendship without requiring either person to give up friends.
- What would transparency look like here?
  For example, volunteering that an ex has made contact rather than hiding it until it is discovered.
- Is this concern based on present behaviour, an earlier relationship experience or both?
  For example, a delayed reply may be ordinary today but evoke a past experience of deception.
- Which agreements would make both people feel more secure and more respected?
  For example, agreeing how overnight travel, social media messages or private one-to-one time with former partners are handled.
- If a boundary is crossed, what repair would restore trust and reduce repetition?
  For example, naming what happened honestly, acknowledging its effect and changing the condition that made it possible.

## Compatibility, renewal and decisions

### Purpose

Compatibility is discovered and built through real life. It is more than early attraction or a paper match: it is the degree to which two people can create a relationship that is mutually enjoyable, stable, fair and capable of facing reality together.

### Chemistry and compatibility

Initial chemistry establishes that attraction exists. It does not establish shared direction, kindness, reliability, sexual compatibility over time, emotional availability or an ability to repair. Those qualities emerge as two people know one another across ordinary life, disappointment, difference, boredom, stress and change.

Over time, attraction can become more personal and distinctive. A relationship becomes more valuable not merely because it has lasted, but because the people have developed knowledge of one another, shared experiences and a bond that cannot be reduced to initial traits.

### What to observe

- Whether the relationship makes ordinary life more enjoyable and expansive for both people.
- Whether major life questions can become a direction both people genuinely choose.
- Whether both people show reliability, kindness, directness and curiosity.
- Whether disagreements produce learning and repair, or a stable destructive pattern.
- Whether affection, desire, appreciation and mutual regard remain part of the relationship.
- Whether either person must repeatedly minimise themselves or carry the relationship alone.

### Renewal versus staying by default

A strained relationship may have a real opportunity for renewal. That requires more than hope, history or a promise to try harder. Both people need to name the conditions that need to change, take observable action and review whether the relationship is actually becoming safer, more connected and more workable.

The relevant comparison is not simply the relationship versus an imagined better partner. It includes the possibility of a good life alone. This makes it easier to judge the relationship by its present reality rather than by fear, sunk cost or potential.

### Practices

- Treat compatibility as something observed across time, not concluded from chemistry or a checklist.
- Discuss the relationship as it actually functions, including what is going well and what each person finds persistently hard.
- When renewal is the question, agree the exact changes needed and what each person will do differently.
- Set a point to review whether those changes have happened, rather than leaving the question indefinitely open.
- Let mutual reality outrank private fantasy about what the relationship could become.

### Questions

- What is true about this relationship now, separate from its history and potential?
  For example, whether conflict currently ends in repair or whether the same injury is repeating.
- Do our major life directions fit together without either person abandoning something essential?
  For example, whether both people can genuinely support the same approach to children, location or commitment.
- Does this relationship make both lives larger, safer and more enjoyable?
  For example, whether each person has more support, play and freedom to pursue meaningful work and friendships.
- If nothing changed, would this still be a relationship each person would choose?
  For example, imagining that the current pattern continues for the next two years.
- What observable changes would demonstrate renewal?
  For example, a specific agreement being kept, a recurring fight reducing or both people regularly feeling more connected.

## Learning from the relationship

### Purpose

A relationship improves when partners learn from its actual patterns. Ideas from books, therapy, podcasts or the wider culture become useful when they help the couple notice what is happening, make a specific change and observe whether it improves the relationship.

### Describe patterns without character verdicts

Start with what reliably happens. For example: “when we are both tired, I become critical, you go quiet, and neither of us returns to the topic.” This gives the couple a shared pattern to address. “You do not care” or “you are impossible” turns a pattern into a character judgement and makes learning less likely.

The pattern may include each person’s contribution, the circumstances that make it more likely, the meaning each person makes of it and the responses that keep it alive.

### A simple learning loop

1. Describe the recurring pattern without assigning character labels.
2. Identify the needs, fears or conditions involved.
3. Agree one small change that either person can make.
4. Try it in the relevant situation.
5. Review its effect on connection, trust, enjoyment or repetition.
6. Keep, adapt or replace the practice together.

Small changes make it easier to learn what helps. They also turn broad intentions — such as “communicate better” or “be more affectionate” — into behaviour that can actually be noticed.

### Feedback as teaching and learning

Feedback is most useful when it is specific, respectful and connected to a hoped-for change. It asks both people to do difficult work: one person teaches what matters and why; the other learns without converting the feedback into proof of humiliation, rejection or failure.

When defensiveness appears, name the fear underneath it, ask for a concrete example and return to the practical question: what would help next time? This treats feedback as a contribution to the relationship rather than a verdict on character.

### Useful review conversations

### A regular check-in

Use a recurring conversation to ask:

- What has felt good or strengthening recently?
  For example, noticing that a difficult week felt easier because one person made space to listen.
- What have I appreciated about you?
  For example, “I appreciated you taking over dinner when I was exhausted on Tuesday.”
- Is there anything small we should address before it grows?
  For example, a recurring interruption, a missed goodnight or an uneven household task.
- What have we learnt about each other or the relationship?
  For example, realising that one person needs quiet after a social event while the other needs to talk.
- Is there one thing we want to try differently in the next period?
  For example, planning one phone-free evening or raising practical frustrations before the weekend.
- What is each person’s current capacity, and what would reduce unnecessary pressure this week?
  For example, one person is at twenty per cent after a work deadline, so the couple cancels optional plans and orders dinner.
- Is there a fact we are both observing but a different story each of us is telling about it?
  For example, both notice fewer messages during the day, while one sees distance and the other sees a demanding work week.

### After a recurring difficulty

- What is the pattern, stated in observable terms?
  For example, “when money comes up after dinner, one of us gets sharp and the other stops talking.”
- When does it happen most often?
  For example, late at night, after social events or when either person has had a difficult workday.
- What does each person make it mean?
  For example, one hears criticism while the other hears avoidance.
- What has each person already tried?
  For example, taking a break, sending a text or trying to settle the issue immediately.
- What is the smallest useful experiment now?
  For example, discuss money on Saturday morning for twenty minutes with both phones away.
- When will we revisit the result?
  For example, agree to ask at the next weekly check-in whether the new approach reduced tension.

### Practices

- Keep feedback specific, current and connected to a request.
- Prefer a small testable change to a large declaration of intent.
- Review progress together; do not silently treat an agreement as permanent if it is not working.
- Keep learning about the relationship as both people and their circumstances change.
- Use the other model notes to decide whether a pattern concerns security, maintenance, repair, boundaries or compatibility.
- Include one personal responsibility in a review: what each person will change in how they show up, not only what they want from the other.

## Operating rhythm

- **Ordinary weeks:** make space for attention, appreciation, intimacy, shared activity and genuine curiosity.
- **When friction appears:** raise it early; describe the concrete event and its meaning; make a request rather than a global accusation.
- **When stress takes over:** pause long enough to regulate, agree when to return and approach the issue from the same side.
- **After a rupture:** acknowledge impact, reconnect and agree what will be different next time.
- **Periodically:** revisit shared direction, agreements, boundaries and whether the relationship is receiving enough care.
